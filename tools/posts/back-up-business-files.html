The question is not whether a laptop will fail, get stolen or catch ransomware, but when. We regularly see business owners lose years of invoices, client files and photos because everything lived on one computer. A good backup turns that disaster into a minor inconvenience. It does not need to be expensive or complicated.

The 3-2-1 rule

IT professionals use a simple rule to judge whether data is safe:

  • 3 copies of your important files: the original plus two backups.
  • 2 different types of storage, for example your laptop and an external drive, or your laptop and a cloud service.
  • 1 copy kept somewhere else, away from your office or home, so a fire, burglary or flood does not take everything at once.

For most small businesses, that means files on the computer, a cloud backup and an external drive.

Step 1: Decide what needs backing up

Make a list of what would hurt to lose. For most businesses it is:

  • Accounting files and invoices (or make sure your accounting software is online and exportable)
  • Client documents, contracts and quotes
  • Photos and videos, especially for photographers, builders and anyone who documents their work
  • Email, if it is stored only on one computer
  • Passwords, if they are kept in a file instead of a password manager

Programs such as Microsoft Office can be reinstalled, so they do not need backing up. Your files are what matter.

Step 2: Set up a cloud backup

Cloud storage copies your files to a secure data centre automatically. The most common options are OneDrive (included with Microsoft 365), Google Drive and Dropbox.

  1. Choose one service and sign in with a business account, not a personal one that a staff member will take with them.
  2. Turn on folder backup for Desktop, Documents and Pictures. On Windows, OneDrive calls this "Back up important PC folders".
  3. Check you have enough storage. Free plans fill up fast; paid plans are usually affordable for a small team.
  4. Make sure files show as synced (a tick or cloud icon) rather than stuck or paused.
Cloud sync is not a full backup on its own. If a file is deleted or encrypted by ransomware on your laptop, the change can sync to the cloud too. Use the service's version history or recycle bin to recover, and keep an offline copy as well.

Step 3: Add an external drive

An external hard drive or SSD gives you a copy that is not connected to the internet, which ransomware cannot reach while the drive is unplugged.

  • Windows: use File History (Settings, then search "File History") to back up your folders to the drive automatically while it is connected.
  • Mac: use Time Machine, which keeps hourly, daily and weekly copies.
  • Plug the drive in once a week, let the backup finish, then unplug it and store it safely, ideally at a different location from the computer.

Some businesses keep two drives and swap them: one at the office, one at home.

Step 4: Test that you can get files back

A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a plan. Every few months:

  1. Pick a few files at random, such as an old invoice or a photo.
  2. Restore them from the cloud version history and from the external drive.
  3. Open them and check they are complete and not corrupted.

It takes ten minutes and tells you whether your backup will work on the day you need it.

Power cuts and surges

Power cuts and the surges when power returns are hard on hard drives and routers. Save your work regularly, use a surge protector on computers and network equipment, and let laptops run on battery during short outages rather than unplugging and replugging repeatedly. A small UPS for the router and fibre box keeps the internet (and cloud backup) running during short cuts.

A simple backup routine

  • Every day: files sync to the cloud automatically. Glance at the icon to check it is not paused.
  • Every week: connect the external drive, let the backup finish, unplug it.
  • Every few months: test a restore.
  • When someone leaves: make sure their work files are in a business account, not their personal one.

Common questions

Is a USB flash drive good enough?

Flash drives are easy to lose and can fail without warning. They are fine for carrying a file to a meeting, but use an external hard drive or SSD for backups, together with cloud storage.

My computer died. Can my files be recovered?

Often, yes. If the computer will not start, the drive inside is frequently still readable. Stop using the device, do not let anyone format it, and get it checked. The sooner it is looked at, the better the chance of recovery.

Do I need to back up files stored in Google Drive or OneDrive?

For most small businesses, the service's built-in version history and recycle bin are enough protection against accidents. For critical records, an occasional export to an external drive gives you a copy outside that account too, in case it is locked or compromised.
