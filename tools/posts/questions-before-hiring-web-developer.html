Many of the business owners who contact us already have a website, but cannot update it, cannot reach the person who built it, or discover their domain is registered to someone else. Almost all of those problems could have been avoided with a few questions at the start. Here is what to ask, and what a good answer sounds like.

Ownership and access

1. Whose name will the domain, hosting and email be registered in?

Good answer: "Yours." The domain, hosting account and business email should be registered to you or your business, even if the developer sets them up and manages them. You should be able to log in or get the login details whenever you ask.

2. Who owns the website once it is paid for?

Good answer: "You do, once the final invoice is paid," with this written in the quote or terms. Ask what you will receive at handover: login details, files, and any admin passwords.

3. Can I move the website to another developer or host later?

Some builders lock you into their own platform, which makes moving difficult or impossible. That can be acceptable for a simple, cheap site, but you should know before you start.

Scope and price

4. Exactly what is included?

Ask for an itemised quote that lists the pages, features (such as bookings, payments or an admin dashboard), domain, hosting, email and setup. A single number with no detail makes it hard to compare quotes or know what you are entitled to. Our guide on what affects the cost of a website explains the main cost drivers.

5. What are the once-off costs and the monthly or yearly costs?

Every website has running costs, at minimum the domain renewal and hosting. Ask what they are, who pays them, and whether maintenance is included or charged separately.

6. How do payments work?

A deposit to start and the balance on completion is common and fair for both sides. Be cautious about paying everything upfront for a large project with someone you have not worked with before.

Process and timeline

7. How long will it take, and what do you need from me?

A good developer gives a realistic timeline and tells you what they need: your logo, text, photos, price list and logins. Delays usually come from missing content, so ask early what is required.

8. Will I see the website while it is being built?

Ask whether you will get a preview link to follow progress. Seeing the work as it happens prevents surprises at the end, when changes are more expensive.

9. How many rounds of changes are included?

Most developers include a set number of revision rounds. Knowing this upfront avoids arguments later, and encourages you to collect your feedback into one clear list.

Quality and results

10. Can I see websites you have built and are still live?

Look at their recent work on your phone, not just in screenshots. Is it fast? Easy to use? Does it work properly on mobile? If possible, ask one of their clients how the experience was.

11. Will the website be mobile-friendly and set up for Google?

Most of your visitors will be on phones, so this is not optional. Basic SEO setup should include page titles and descriptions, a sitemap, Google Search Console and connecting your Google Business Profile.

After launch

12. What happens if something breaks or I need changes?

Ask how long they fix bugs for free after launch, how quickly they respond, and what they charge for updates or a monthly maintenance plan. Ask whether you will be able to make simple changes yourself, such as prices, photos and specials.

Red flags to watch for

  • They will not put the domain in your name.
  • No written quote or terms, just a WhatsApp message with a price.
  • Promises of "guaranteed first place on Google". Nobody can guarantee rankings.
  • No live websites to show you.
  • Pressure to pay everything immediately.

Common questions

Is a freelancer or an agency better?

Either can be excellent. What matters is their recent work, how clearly they communicate, and whether they answer the questions above well. Freelancers are often more affordable and personal; larger agencies can handle very big projects.

Should I use a website builder instead?

Builders such as Wix or Squarespace can work if you have time to set up and maintain the site yourself. A developer makes more sense when you need custom features, want it done properly the first time, or would rather spend your time running the business.

What should be in a website contract or terms?

At minimum: what is included, the price and payment schedule, the timeline, the number of revisions, who owns the website and domain, what happens after launch, and how either side can end the agreement.
