When a small business's email, Facebook page or accounting software is taken over, the cause is almost always the same: a weak, reused or stolen password, and no second step to stop the attacker. Fixing this does not need expensive software. It needs a password manager, two-step verification and a few good habits.

Why reused passwords are the biggest risk

Websites get breached all the time, and the lists of email addresses and passwords end up for sale. Criminals then try those same details on email, banking, social media and shopping sites automatically. If you use the same password in several places, one breach anywhere opens all of them.

So the most important rule is not "make it complicated". It is use a different password for every account.

Step 1: Use a password manager

Nobody can remember fifty unique passwords. A password manager remembers them for you, fills them in on websites and apps, and generates strong new ones. You only remember one strong master password.

  • Popular options include Bitwarden, 1Password and the managers built into Google Chrome and Apple devices.
  • For a team, choose one that lets you share specific logins with specific staff members.
  • Make the master password a long passphrase, such as four or five unrelated words, and never use it anywhere else.
Start with your most important accounts: email, banking, accounting software, your domain and hosting account, and social media. Change each to a unique password stored in the manager.

Step 2: Turn on two-step verification everywhere it matters

Two-step verification (also called 2FA or multi-factor authentication) asks for a second proof when someone logs in from a new device, usually a code from an app or a prompt on your phone. Even if your password is stolen, the attacker cannot get in without your phone.

Turn it on for:

  1. Your business email (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 or your host's email)
  2. Online banking (usually already required)
  3. Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp (WhatsApp calls it "two-step verification" with a PIN)
  4. Accounting and invoicing software
  5. Your domain registrar and hosting account

Which type of second step is best?

  • Authenticator apps (such as Google Authenticator or Microsoft Authenticator) are a good balance of security and convenience.
  • SMS codes are better than nothing, but can be intercepted through SIM-swap fraud.
  • Passkeys are newer and let you sign in with your phone's fingerprint or face unlock instead of a password. Use them where offered.

Save the backup codes each service gives you when you turn on 2FA, in your password manager or printed and locked away. They get you back in if you lose your phone.

Step 3: Stop sharing one login

Many small businesses have one email login or one accounting login that everyone uses. When someone leaves, the password often never changes. Where possible:

  • Give each person their own account, so you can remove one person without disrupting everyone.
  • Where a shared login is unavoidable, share it through the password manager rather than WhatsApp or a sticky note, and change it when someone leaves.
  • Use shared mailboxes or aliases (like info@ or accounts@) instead of sharing one person's mailbox.

Step 4: A checklist for when staff leave

  1. Disable their email account, or change its password and forward their mail to someone else.
  2. Remove them from shared password vaults, cloud folders and social media page roles.
  3. Change any shared passwords they knew, including Wi-Fi.
  4. Make sure work files they stored are in a business account you control.
  5. Retrieve company laptops and phones, and sign them out of business accounts.

Step 5: Watch for warning signs

  • Login alerts from a device or place you do not recognise
  • Password reset emails you did not request
  • Sent emails or posts you did not write
  • New forwarding rules in your email settings

If you see any of these, change the password immediately from a trusted device, sign out other sessions and check your 2FA settings. Our guide on spotting phishing and invoice scams explains what to do next.

Common questions

Is it safe to store passwords in a password manager?

Reputable password managers encrypt your vault so that only your master password can unlock it. It is far safer than reusing passwords, writing them in a notebook or saving them in a document on your desktop.

How often should I change my passwords?

Current guidance is to change a password when there is a reason to, such as a breach, a staff member leaving or a suspicious login, rather than on a fixed schedule. A unique, strong password with 2FA does not need regular changes.

What if I lose my phone with my authenticator app?

Use the backup codes you saved to sign in and set up the app on your new phone. Some authenticator apps also offer secure cloud backup of your codes. Set this up before you need it.
