Most cyber attacks on small businesses do not involve clever hacking. They involve a convincing email, SMS or WhatsApp message that gets someone to click a link, enter a password or pay into the wrong account. The good news: once you know the signs, most scams are easy to spot.

The scams we see most often

"We have changed our banking details"

This is one of the most expensive scams for small businesses. A supplier's email account is hacked, or a look-alike address is used, and an email arrives saying their bank account has changed. The next invoice is paid into the criminal's account. The email often continues a real conversation, so it looks completely genuine.

Never change a supplier's banking details based on an email or WhatsApp alone. Phone the supplier on a number you already have (not the one in the email) and confirm the change with someone you know.

Fake invoices and "proof of payment"

An email arrives with an attachment called "Invoice" or "POP" from someone you do not expect. The attachment or link leads to a fake login page or installs malware.

Fake login pages

"Your mailbox is full", "Your Microsoft 365 password expires today" or "A document has been shared with you". The link leads to a page that looks exactly like a real login screen and collects your password.

Delivery and tax SMS scams

Messages claiming a parcel is waiting for a small customs fee, or that you have a tax refund to claim, with a link to "pay" or "verify". Courier companies and the tax authority do not collect fees or banking details through random SMS links.

Seven warning signs

  1. Urgency or pressure: "Pay today or your account will be suspended", "Final notice".
  2. A sender address that is slightly wrong: an extra letter, a different ending, or a free email address for a "company".
  3. A link that does not match: on a computer, hover over the link to see where it really goes. On a phone, press and hold to preview it.
  4. Unexpected attachments, especially zip files, or documents that ask you to "enable editing" or "enable content".
  5. Requests for passwords, OTPs or banking details. Legitimate companies never ask for your one-time PIN.
  6. Changes to payment details, however small.
  7. Something that feels off: an unusual greeting, poor spelling from a company that normally writes well, or a tone that does not match the person you know.

Protect your business

Turn on two-step verification

Two-step verification (also called 2FA or multi-factor authentication) means a stolen password is not enough to get into your email. Turn it on for business email, banking, accounting software and social media accounts. An authenticator app is safer than SMS codes.

Use a separate password for every account

When one site is breached, criminals try the same email and password everywhere else. A password manager remembers unique passwords for you.

Set a payment rule for the whole team

Agree that nobody pays a new supplier, or pays into changed banking details, without a phone call to verify and a second person approving. Write it down, so staff feel confident saying no to "urgent" requests.

Keep devices updated

Updates fix the security holes that malicious attachments use. Let Windows, macOS, phones and browsers update automatically, and restart when asked.

What to do if you clicked

  1. Do not panic, but act quickly.
  2. If you entered a password, change it immediately from a device you trust, and change it anywhere else you used the same password. Turn on two-step verification.
  3. If you made a payment or shared banking details, phone your bank's fraud line straight away. The faster they know, the better the chance of stopping the money.
  4. If you opened an attachment, disconnect the computer from the internet and run a full virus scan, or get it checked. Our guide on removing a virus from Windows covers the steps.
  5. Check your email settings for rules or forwarding addresses you did not create. Attackers often add these to secretly copy your mail.
  6. Warn your contacts if your account sent messages you did not write.

Common questions

Can an email hack my computer just by opening it?

Simply reading an email in a modern, updated email app is very unlikely to infect your computer. The danger is clicking links, opening attachments and entering passwords. Keep your software updated, and be careful with what you click.

How do I check if my email address has been in a data breach?

The free website Have I Been Pwned lets you enter your email address and see which known breaches it appeared in. If your address shows up, change the password for those services and anywhere you reused it.

Should I report phishing emails?

Yes. Use the "Report phishing" or "Report spam" option in Gmail or Outlook, which helps protect other people. If the scam pretends to be your bank, forward it to your bank's fraud or phishing address, which is listed on their website.
