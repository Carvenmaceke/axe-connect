A laptop that will not charge does not always need a new battery. Before you spend money, rule out the simple causes: the socket, the charger, a battery setting, or a worn charging port. These checks go from easiest to hardest.

Safety first: if the battery is swollen, the case is bulging or the touchpad is being pushed up, stop using and charging the laptop. A swollen battery is a fire risk and needs to be replaced.

1. Check the power at the wall

It sounds obvious, but try a different wall socket and remove any multiplug or extension lead. Plugs and multiplugs get damaged over time, and after load shedding or power surges a socket can stop working while others in the room are fine. If your charger has a light on the brick or at the tip, check whether it comes on.

2. Check the whole charger

Most laptop chargers have two cables: one from the wall to the power brick, and one from the brick to the laptop. Look along both for kinks, frayed sections or bent pins, and make sure the cable is pushed firmly into the brick. If you have a friend with the same laptop model, borrowing their charger for a few minutes is the quickest test there is.

3. Use the right charger

A charger with a lower wattage than the original may run the laptop but never charge the battery, or charge very slowly. The wattage is printed on the power brick (for example 45 W, 65 W or 90 W). For laptops that charge over USB-C, a phone charger is usually not strong enough; you need a USB-C charger that supports the laptop's wattage. Cheap unbranded chargers are a common cause of charging problems and can damage the laptop.

4. Look for a battery-saving setting

If Windows says "Plugged in, not charging" at around 60% or 80%, the laptop may be deliberately stopping there to protect the battery. Many manufacturers include this feature in their own app:

  • Lenovo: Conservation mode in Lenovo Vantage
  • Dell: battery settings in Dell Power Manager or Dell Optimizer
  • ASUS: Battery Health Charging in MyASUS
  • HP: battery care settings in the BIOS or HP's support app on some models

If the setting is on, that is normal behaviour, not a fault. Switch it off when you need a full charge for a long day away from a plug.

5. Check the battery's health

Windows can produce a battery report. Open the Start menu, type cmd, open Command Prompt and run:

powercfg /batteryreport

It saves a file called battery-report.html and tells you where. Open it and compare Design capacity with Full charge capacity. If the full charge capacity is a small fraction of the design capacity, the battery is worn out and will not hold a charge for long, even when it charges normally.

6. Restart and update

A full restart (not shut down) and installing waiting Windows updates fixes some charging problems caused by power management drivers. If your manufacturer's support app offers a BIOS or firmware update, install that too while the laptop is plugged in and running on mains power.

7. Check the charging port

If the charger only works when you hold the plug at a certain angle, or the plug feels loose in the laptop, the charging port is likely worn or damaged. This is common on laptops that are often used while charging on a lap or bed. Do not keep forcing it: a damaged port can short and damage the motherboard. This is a repair for a technician.

What it usually comes down to

  • Charger light off on every socket: the charger is faulty. Replace it with the correct model and wattage.
  • Stops at 60–80%: a battery-saving setting.
  • Charges, but dies quickly: a worn battery. Check the battery report.
  • Works only at an angle: a damaged charging port.
